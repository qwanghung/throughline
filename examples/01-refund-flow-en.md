# Example: refund flow, spoken 3-min pitch (English)

> SYNTHETIC: written to show the shape, not from a real project. Replace it with your own before/after pairs. The facts here are invented for illustration. The skill itself must never invent facts.

**Audience card (short)**
- Decider: Head of Retail Ops (client)
- Knows by heart: store operations, refund policy, staff turnover, the POS hardware
- Cares about: queue time at peak, refund errors, cost of training new cashiers
- Worry: "Retraining 400 cashiers again?"
- Wants from this minute: a decision (approve a pilot)
- Through line: a cashier can finish any normal refund alone

## Before

> In today's fast-paced retail landscape, the refund experience plays a crucial role in customer satisfaction. Our team conducted extensive research, including interviews and workshops, to deeply understand the pain points. We discovered that the current flow isn't just slow — it's fundamentally broken. Inspired by the principles of human-centered design, we created a seamless, intuitive and delightful new refund journey that empowers cashiers. Key features include smart receipt lookup, a streamlined approval process, and a modern UI. We believe this will transform the way your stores handle refunds. Let us know your thoughts!

What's wrong:
- Opens with context-setting the decider knows by heart ("fast-paced retail landscape").
- Process narration with no numbers ("extensive research").
- "Isn't just slow — it's fundamentally broken" rejects something nobody said.
- The principle ("human-centered design") produces no decision. It's a slogan.
- Sales language: "seamless, intuitive and delightful" (also a forced triad).
- "Modern UI" maps to no problem.
- No hook, no through line, no alternatives, no gaps, no ask.

## After

> What if a refund never needed a manager? I'm asking for one store to pilot the new refund flow in March.
>
> Today a refund takes four minutes at the till, and at peak the queue behind it grows by six people. In our sessions, 5 of 8 cashiers had to call a manager because they couldn't find the original receipt. And last quarter 3% of refunds went to the wrong payment method, which finance then fixed by hand.
>
> The flow borrows from airline check-in: start from what the customer has, not from what the system needs. So the cashier scans the card or the phone number first, and the receipt appears. No receipt number, no manager call. Approval only interrupts for refunds over the limit, and the refund defaults to the original payment method.
>
> That fixes the lookup and the wrong-method errors outright. Queue time should drop, but we've only timed it in the prototype: 1 minute 40 on average, not yet in a real store. That's what the pilot is for.
>
> We looked at adding a manager PIN shortcut instead. It was cheaper, but it kept the manager in every refund, so it didn't touch the queue.
>
> On retraining: the flow keeps the existing till layout. New cashiers in testing finished a refund unaided on the first try.
>
> So: one store, March, and we report queue time and error rate after four weeks.
