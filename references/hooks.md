# Hooks

The hook is the first thing the audience reads or hears. Usually that's the **title of the slide or the title of the document**. It has one job: plant one question in a specific person's head and hold it open long enough that they want the next line. It should work like the first breath of an elevator pitch or the first ten seconds of a good TED-style talk.

## What every good hook does

1. **Context first.** The right reader knows instantly that it's for them. A provocative line with no context reads as nonsense, not curiosity.
2. **Their world, not yours.** Lead with their pain or benefit, in their words. The audience is the hero; the product or design is the gift, not the star.
3. **A turn.** Something breaks an expectation: a contrast, a challenged belief, an ideal that sounds impossible.
4. **Withhold the answer.** It's a trailer, not the movie. Tease the outcome. Don't explain the solution, and don't let them guess the answer from the hook itself.
5. **Compress.** Short words, short sentences, one breath. The most value per word.
6. **Pay it off.** The promise is real, and what follows delivers it.

## What to know first

- **The audience card** (`audience.md`): what they care about, what they know by heart.
- **The through line or conviction**: what the hook leads into.
- **Their world.** If the card doesn't cover it, ask up to three questions, starting with the most useful:
  1. What field are they in, and what do they talk about? (Their trade words, heroes, running jokes.)
  2. What's on their mind right now: a number they're chasing, a fear, a recent win or failure?
  3. What have they heard a hundred times and are tired of? (So the hook avoids it.)

  Offer a guess to react to: "For hotel GMs I'd guess occupancy, reviews and staff turnover. Right?"

## What to deliver

By default, give **both** forms:

- **One-line hooks**: 3–5 candidates for the title.
- **Three-beat openings**: 2–3 candidates for the first lines of the doc, or the first slides of the deck.

Watch which form the designer picks. If they keep choosing one form, write that under "Taste notes" in the plan and lead with that form in later runs, while still offering the other.

### One-line hook

A single line that can stand as a title.

- **Slide title:** a full-sentence claim or question, 3–10 words. It carries the point; the slide body carries the evidence.
- **Doc title:** short, 2–6 words. Pair it with a **subtitle**, one sentence that does the one-liner's job (below).

### Three-beat opening

Three short lines that build and hold a curiosity loop:

1. **Context**: what this is about, and why it touches them (common ground, their pain, their benefit).
2. **Turn**: the line that breaks the expectation. Often a "but", a contrast, or a challenged belief.
3. **Reveal**: the direction the rest of the piece takes, teased, not explained. It's still on topic, and the piece must deliver it.

> Context: "Every design review starts with a beautiful deck."
> Turn: "By slide three, half the room is on Slack."
> Reveal: "What wins the room is one thread every slide hangs on."

On slides, give each beat its own slide, one line per slide. In a doc, the three beats are the opening paragraph, or the title, subtitle and first line.

### Close the loop or hold it

Choose by format:

| Format | Loop | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Tagline, bio, doc subtitle, README one-liner | **Close** it in the same sentence | **Problem → named solution → result**: one problem, your named solution, the result that resolves the tension |
| Doc title, slide title, talk opening, doc intro | **Hold** it open | Trailer: context, a turn, the outcome teased. Don't name or describe the product yet |

One-liner example (closes): "Designers lose rooms they should win. throughline plans the story with you, so the room stays with you."

### Elevator-length opening (when asked)

For a 30-second pitch, use the trailer in three acts:
1. **Their ordinary world**: the job they're trying to get done.
2. **The trigger**: what changed, so the usual alternatives stop working.
3. **The tease**: the better way, as an outcome, without naming the product.

## Opening types

Draft across several types, not variations of one.

| Type | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bold claim** | Sounds wrong; the piece earns it | "Ideas don't matter, and this little car proves it." (Wojtek Dziedzic, Config 2025) |
| **Challenged belief** | Says the opposite of what their field assumes | "Lean at 45, and I eat carbs every day." |
| **What if** | An ideal outcome, stated as possible | "What if a refund never needed a manager?" |
| **Benefit first** | Their goal, then your idea as the way there | "If you want better sleep, you need magnesium." |
| **Number** | One concrete, true, surprising figure | "Eighty-eight percent of our new users are gone in three months." |
| **Scene** | Drops them into a moment, no setup | "Slide three. Your stakeholder is answering Slack." |
| **Confession** | A small admission that earns trust | "I designed this app, and I didn't know import existed." |
| **Contrast** | Two facts that shouldn't both be true | "Everyone sees the import button. Twelve percent use it." |
| **Imaginative** | Whimsical and sensory, for craft audiences | "What if work could bloom the way sunlight does when it slips through glass?" |

Match the register to the audience:
- **Business deciders:** number, contrast, what-if, benefit first.
- **Craft audiences:** imaginative, scene, confession.
- **Educational talks:** scene, confession, bold claim.

## Style limits

- **Creator-style phrasing only on request.** Don't use social-video moves unless the designer asks for them: "but get this", "don't do Y, do Z", "this should not work", "you won't believe", stacked teasers. By default, the turn is stated plainly in the piece's own register.
- **Pop-culture references: rare, and chosen by the designer.** Use at most one per piece. Before using one, ask what references the audience shares, or offer one to approve. If the designer names one, ask for a link (Threads, YouTube, Facebook) or a one-line explanation of what it means to the audience. Never guess what a reference means. For Vietnamese audiences, ask for local references rather than defaulting to Western ones.

## Rules

- **Short.** A slide title is 10 words or fewer, a doc title 6 or fewer, and a one-line hook 12 or fewer. Every line is sayable in one breath.
- **One idea.** If it needs a comma to survive, it's two hooks.
- **Concrete.** A thing, a person, a number, a moment. "Users struggle with onboarding" is a topic, not a hook.
- **In their words.** Their trade vocabulary and their units.
- **Honest.** A what-if or imaginative hook may picture the ideal. A number or claim must be true, and nothing after it may walk it back.
- **Universal.** Any member of the audience can picture it without the speaker's backstory (`spine.md` § Universality test).
- **No throat-clearing.** Never "Today I want to talk about", "In today's fast-paced world", "Did you know…?", "Imagine a world where", "Who can relate?", or a definition. In Vietnamese, avoid "Bạn có biết…?", "Hãy cùng…", "Trong bối cảnh…" (`patterns-vi.md`).

## Score the candidates

Score each candidate 0–2 on seven tests.

| Test | 2 means |
|---|---|
| **Context** | The right reader knows instantly it's for them |
| **Stop** | They would look up from their phone |
| **Theirs** | It's about their world and their pain or benefit, in their words |
| **Gap** | It plants one question and doesn't give away the answer |
| **Short** | Within the length rule, one breath |
| **True** | Nothing after it has to correct it, and the piece pays it off |
| **Bridge** | One sentence connects it to the through line |

Drop any candidate that scores 0 on Context, True or Theirs, whatever its total.

## Present the hooks

For each finalist:
- the hook, or the three beats
- its type, and whether it closes or holds the loop
- the **bridge**: the sentence that follows and connects it to the point (for a one-line hook)
- one line on why it fits this audience
- its score

Then ask the designer to pick, mix or veto. Record vetoes and reasons in the plan, and write the next round in that direction.

## Say it aloud

Read the hook and its bridge aloud. If you run out of breath, stumble, or it sounds like an ad, cut or rewrite it. A good title can stand alone for a beat of silence.
