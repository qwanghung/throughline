# Density: value per minute

**VPM = relevance × clarity ÷ noise**

The same idea told with more noise has less impact. To raise VPM, aim better, structure more clearly and cut noise. More vocabulary doesn't help.

The listener should finish knowing four things:
- what you mean
- what you want
- what they need to do
- what matters

## Level 1: Discern the ask

Before drafting, ask: *what would make this minute valuable to the decider?*

The answer is usually one of: a decision, a recommendation, reassurance, alignment, the next step, or feeling understood. Record it on the audience card. It sets the point, the proof and the push.

The low-VPM habit is talking to yourself out loud. The high-VPM habit is talking to the person in front of you about what they want.

## Level 2: Deliver the payload early

- The point comes by the second sentence, never after the background.
- Use **point → proof → push**, one sentence each, then stop.
- Example: "We're on track. Two risks: approval and the deadline. I'm handling both today."
- Give the minimum proof that supports the point. More evidence lives in the appendix or the Q&A.

## Level 3: Cut the noise

Noise is anything that doesn't help the listener understand, decide or act.

**Rule: if it doesn't change the decision, remove it.**

Common noise:
- long stories that don't carry the through line
- hedge openers ("I'm not an expert, but…", "this might be wrong, but…")
- over-apologising
- over-explaining
- the same point repeated in different words
- every possible edge case (move them to the appendix)
- thinking aloud, circling the point before landing on it

Replace stacked hedges with one calibrated confidence statement: "My recommendation is to ship it this week. I'm about 70% confident; the risk is approval."

Cutting means sacrificing material you like. Expect to cut good work. That's the point.

## Tests

Apply them in order. Cut any sentence that fails one.

1. **Decision test**: does it change what the decider will decide or do? If not, cut it.
2. **Known test**: is it on the audience card's "knows by heart" list? Cut it.
3. **Through-line test**: does it serve the through line (`spine.md` §0)? If not, cut it.
4. **Skip test**: read the passage without it. If nothing is lost, cut it.
5. **Anyone test**: could it appear unchanged in another designer's pitch for another product? Replace it with the specific detail or cut it.
6. **Depth test**: one level below the audience's baseline is fine. Two or more levels below, cut it.

## Stories vs noise

A story engages the audience, and a long story is noise. Keep a story only when all of these hold:
- it carries the through line
- it explains faster than the abstract version would
- it fits in a few sentences

Use one per pitch at most.

## Scoring (review mode)

Score each section from 1 to 10 on relevance (R), clarity (C) and noise (N), then compute R × C ÷ N. Report the three lowest-scoring sections with a suggested cut for each. This is a rough ranking of where to cut first, not a precise metric.

## Budgets

- One idea per paragraph or slide.
- Process: at most one sentence, and only if it builds trust ("we tested with 8 store managers").
- Background: at most one sentence, and only what the audience might not share.
- Each problem: 2–3 sentences. Each feature: 2–3 sentences.
- Numbers: round them for speech, keep them exact in writing.

## Move, don't cut

Detail that's noise for one reader can be the point for another. In the dry run, how-it's-drawn detail looked like over-explanation until the audience turned out to want to clone the work. Before cutting a passage, ask whether a second reader on the card needs it. If one does, move it to a layer they can open (a "Build it" toggle, an appendix, a footnote) and keep the main read clean.

## Don't cut

- A hedge that reflects real uncertainty, best said as a confidence level.
- The strongest objection and your answer to it.
- The "not solved" rows of the map.

## Shortcut phrases

These force you to the point when speaking:

| English | Tiếng Việt |
|---|---|
| "Short answer: …" | "Nói ngắn gọn: …" |
| "My recommendation is …" | "Em đề xuất …" / "Mình đề xuất …" |
| "The real issue is …" | "Vấn đề thật sự là …" |
| "The next step is …" | "Bước tiếp theo là …" |
| "Do you want my recommendation, or the options?" | "Anh/chị muốn nghe đề xuất của em, hay các phương án?" |

Use them freely in speech. In writing, use them sparingly: if every paragraph opens with one, they become a tell of their own.
